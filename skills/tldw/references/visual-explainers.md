# Visual explainers

Design for understanding: the image should help the reader see a relationship,
mechanism, or distinction that prose alone makes harder to grasp.

## Choose what to show

Distill the source into its central point, the explanation supporting it, and any
example or caveat needed to interpret it. Give each image one learning objective.
Use one image when sufficient; for layered material, introduce the central idea
before adding only the mechanism, example, or consequence that needs its own space.
Do not force every source into the same number of panels or sequence of topics.

Choose a visual structure that carries meaning:

- A process or causal chain: show concrete stages and label what changes between
  them. Arrows must mean a stated relationship, not imply unsupported causation.
- A comparison or tradeoff: show the same example under contrasting conditions,
  with comparable scales and the decisive difference made visible.
- A system or mechanism: use an annotated scene, cutaway, or exploded view to
  reveal how the relevant parts interact.
- A sequence or abstract idea: use a short storyboard or a concrete analogy.
  Make added examples recognizably illustrative; do not present them as events
  or evidence from the source.

Preserve important qualifiers and attribution: a speaker's claim, forecast, or
opinion must not become an established fact. Use only supplied numerical evidence
for charts; do not invent percentages, trends, scale, or precision for decoration.

## Compose and generate

Choose a subject-appropriate visual treatment: an expressive editorial
illustration, an annotated scene, a clean technical drawing, or a playful
storyboard when it helps the explanation. Follow a requested style. Use a strong
focal point, deliberate contrast, breathing room, and a clear reading order.
Interest should come from the subject and composition, not unrelated decoration.

Prefer a takeaway headline and short labels attached to the relevant objects.
Avoid a wall of bullets, generic icon grids, repeated rounded boxes, or the same
gradient-card template for every topic. Labels should clarify the illustration;
the illustration should do more than decorate labels. Keep type legible at the
displayed size; move precision-heavy detail into accompanying text when needed.

For each built-in image-generation call, specify the intended reader, learning
objective, exact relationships to depict, composition, style, and quoted text.
Include the necessary source facts and caveats in the prompt; do not expect the
image tool to infer them from the rest of the conversation. Keep a consistent
palette, typography, and recurring visual language across a sequence, while
varying composition to fit each idea.

## Review

Inspect each result for a clear takeaway, readable and accurate text, correct
relationships, and source fidelity. Check arrow directions, labels, numerical
values, comparative scale, and caveats. Simplify, split, or regenerate an image
that obscures the explanation. Make targeted corrections while preserving the
rest of the sequence. Show the verified images in their intended reading order.
